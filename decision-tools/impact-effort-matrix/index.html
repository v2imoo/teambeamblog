Impact–Effort Matrix

Prioritise by plotting the payoff against the work required.

Contents

Overview

The Impact–Effort Matrix plots each candidate action on two axes: how much impact it would have and how much effort it would take. Four zones emerge, and the map instantly separates the obvious wins from the traps.

The point is to resist the gravity of low-impact busywork that feels productive, and to be honest that some high-impact ideas are simply too expensive to do now. It rewards the quadrant everyone underuses: high impact, low effort — the quick wins that build momentum.

When to use it

A backlog of possible actions and limited time — features, fixes, experiments, marketing bets.

How to use it

Step 1

Quick wins — high impact, low effort

Do these first. They build momentum and credibility.

Step 2

Big bets — high impact, high effort

Worth doing, but plan and sequence them; you can only run a few at once.

Step 3

Fill-ins — low impact, low effort

Do when you have slack, or delegate. Never let these crowd out big bets.

Step 4

Money pits — low impact, high effort

Avoid. These are where roadmaps go to die.

Worked example

A growth team maps twenty ideas. ‘Fix the broken signup email’ is high impact, low effort — a quick win done that afternoon. ‘Rebuild onboarding’ is a big bet, scheduled. ‘Redesign the logo’ turns out low impact, high effort — a money pit, dropped. The map killed three weeks of well-intentioned but pointless work.

Common pitfalls

  • Underestimating effort — big bets often masquerade as quick wins until you start.
  • Overrating impact for ideas you are personally attached to.
  • Living entirely in quick wins and never starting the big bets that move the needle.

Frequently asked questions

How do I estimate impact before doing the work?

Roughly, against your one key metric. Precision is impossible; relative ranking is enough.

What if everything looks like a big bet?

Break big bets into smaller slices until some become quick wins.

Is effort just time?

Time, money, risk and coordination cost — whatever makes something hard to ship.
