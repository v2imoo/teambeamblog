Minto Pyramid

Lead with the answer, then support it — structure that respects the reader’s time.

Contents

Overview

The Minto Pyramid Principle inverts the way most people naturally communicate. Instead of walking the audience through your reasoning to a conclusion at the end, you state the conclusion first, then group your supporting arguments beneath it, then the evidence beneath those — a pyramid with the answer at the apex.

It works because busy readers want the answer immediately and the detail only if they need it. Leading with the conclusion lets them grasp your point in one sentence and choose how deep to go, rather than holding their breath through your thought process.

When to use it

Any high-stakes written or spoken communication — recommendations, reports, executive updates, proposals.

How to use it

Step 1

Start with the answer

Lead with your single main conclusion or recommendation.

Step 2

Group supporting arguments

Beneath it, place the few (usually three) key arguments that support the conclusion.

Step 3

Make groups MECE

Ensure the arguments don’t overlap and together fully support the point.

Step 4

Support each with evidence

Under each argument, place the data and detail that back it.

Step 5

Check the logic flows down

A reader should be able to stop at any level and still have a complete, coherent message.

Worked example

Rather than a report that builds for ten pages to ‘therefore we should exit Market X’, the memo opens: ‘We should exit Market X.’ Then three grouped reasons — shrinking demand, rising cost to serve, better use of the capital — each with its evidence beneath. An executive gets the decision in the first line and drills only where they doubt.

Common pitfalls

  • Burying the conclusion at the end out of habit, forcing readers to dig.
  • Overlapping arguments that make the support feel repetitive and weak.
  • More than a handful of top-level arguments, which readers cannot hold.

Frequently asked questions

Why lead with the conclusion?

Because readers understand and retain your point better when they know where you are going, and busy ones need it fast.

How many supporting arguments?

Usually three — enough to be convincing, few enough to remember.

Does this work for storytelling?

For persuasion and reports, yes. Pure narrative sometimes wants suspense — but business communication rarely does.
