Decision Tools

Six Thinking Hats

Look at a decision from six deliberate perspectives, one at a time.

Contents

Overview

Six Thinking Hats is a method for separating the different modes of thinking that normally collide inside a single conversation. Instead of arguing facts, feelings, risks and ideas all at once, everyone “wears” the same coloured hat at the same time and thinks only in that mode. The result is that a room stops fighting and starts thinking in parallel.

The power of the tool is that it makes thinking a sequence rather than a scramble. Emotion gets its turn, but so does hard data, and the pessimist is not allowed to smother the idea before it has been heard. Because the switching is explicit, people who would normally never volunteer an optimistic case or a gut feeling are given licence to do so.

When to use it

Group discussions that go in circles; solo decisions where one mode of thinking (usually caution or emotion) is drowning out the rest.

How to use it

Step 1

White hat — the facts

State only what is known and what is missing. No interpretation, no opinion. “What data do we actually have, and what would we need?”

Step 2

Red hat — the feelings

Say your gut reaction with no justification required. Hunches and emotions are surfaced instead of hidden.

Step 3

Black hat — the risks

Play devil’s advocate deliberately. Where could this fail, what are the weaknesses, why might it be a bad idea?

Step 4

Yellow hat — the value

The disciplined optimist. What is the best case, the benefit, the reason this could work?

Step 5

Green hat — the alternatives

Generate new ideas, options and creative angles. Quantity over judgement.

Step 6

Blue hat — the process

Manage the thinking itself: set the sequence, keep time, and summarise the conclusion. Usually worn by whoever is facilitating.

Worked example

A product team is split on whether to delay a launch. Under white hat they list the two remaining bugs and the fact that no data exists on churn from a late launch. Red hat reveals half the room is anxious about the reviews. Black hat surfaces the reputational risk of shipping broken. Yellow hat names the revenue already committed. Green hat produces a third option nobody had raised: a limited beta. Blue hat closes on the beta. The decision took twenty minutes instead of a week because the modes never competed.

Common pitfalls

  • Skipping the red hat because it feels unprofessional — the feelings drive the decision anyway; better to name them.
  • Letting the black hat run the whole meeting; caution is one hat of six, not the default.
  • Using it for trivial decisions where the overhead outweighs the benefit.

Frequently asked questions

Do you have to use all six hats?

No — for a quick check you might use only white, black and yellow. But the value comes from forcing the modes you would normally skip, so drop hats consciously, not by habit.

Can one person use it alone?

Yes. Solo, it stops you from letting one mood — usually fear or over-optimism — make the whole decision.

What is the blue hat for?

It is the meta-hat: it decides the order, keeps the group on the current hat, and writes the conclusion.
