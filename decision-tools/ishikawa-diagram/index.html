Ishikawa Diagram

Map the many possible causes of a problem as a fishbone.

Contents

Overview

The Ishikawa (or fishbone, or cause-and-effect) diagram puts the problem at the head of a fish and branches the possible causes off the spine, grouped into categories. It forces a team past the first cause that comes to mind and onto the full landscape of contributing factors before anyone rushes to a fix.

Its strength is breadth. By pre-seeding categories — classically People, Process, Equipment, Materials, Environment, Management — it prompts you to look in places you would otherwise skip, surfacing causes that a single-thread investigation misses.

When to use it

A recurring problem with no single obvious cause — defects, outages, dropping metrics.

How to use it

Step 1

State the problem

Write the specific effect at the head of the fish.

Step 2

Draw the category bones

Add a branch for each cause category relevant to your domain.

Step 3

Brainstorm causes per category

Under each branch, ask what could contribute. Add sub-branches for causes of causes.

Step 4

Ask ‘why’ down each bone

Push each cause deeper, borrowing the 5 Whys, until you reach something actionable.

Step 5

Identify the likely roots

Circle the causes best supported by evidence, and test those first.

Worked example

A factory’s defect rate spikes. The fishbone spreads causes across People (new hires undertrained), Process (a skipped inspection step), Equipment (a worn tool), and Materials (a new supplier). Investigating the most-supported branches, the worn tool and the skipped step turn out to compound. A single-cause hunt would have blamed the new hires and missed both.

Common pitfalls

  • Stopping at symptoms instead of pushing each bone to a root cause.
  • Listing causes with no evidence and treating the diagram as proof.
  • Using generic categories that do not fit your actual process.

Frequently asked questions

What categories should I use?

The classic 6M (People, Methods, Machines, Materials, Measurement, Environment) is a start — adapt them to your domain.

How is it different from 5 Whys?

5 Whys drills one causal chain deep; the fishbone maps many chains wide. They pair well.

Does the diagram prove the cause?

No — it generates candidate causes. You still have to test which ones are real.
